Algorithm selection

Each model from our course answers a different question about a leaf. For “healthy or sick” I compared four families of models.

LOGISTIC REGRESSION

Healthy or sick?

It gives a number between 0 and 1. If the number is above 0.5, we say the leaf is sick. It is simple and fast, but it draws a straight border between the classes.

KNN

Healthy or sick? Which disease?

K-Nearest Neighbors. It finds the K leaves most like the new one, and picks the class that shows up the most. It needs standardized numbers.

RANDOM FOREST

Healthy or sick? (used in the app)

Many decision trees vote. It can follow curved borders between classes and does not need scaling. I expected it to fit these 26 mixed numbers (colors, counts, shares) better than the simple models.

GRADIENT BOOSTING

Healthy or sick? (comparison)

Trees built one after another, each one fixing the mistakes of the last. I added it to check that Random Forest was not leaving accuracy on the table.

K-MEANS

Which part is damaged?

It puts pixels with similar colors into K groups. Green leaf, spots, and background end up in different groups.

Steps: pick K centers. Put each point in the group of the nearest center. Move each center to the middle of its group. Repeat.

LINEAR REGRESSION

How much is damaged?

It draws a line to predict a number: the rust level from 1 to 4.

Q-LEARNING

What is the best action?

An agent learns by trying actions and getting rewards. A table keeps a score for each action in each situation.

Training setup

  • Data. The 26 numbers per photo, made with the automatic leaf cut, the same as the app (file data_grabcut.csv).
  • Split. 1,248 photos (312 plants) to train and 312 photos (78 plants) for the final test, split by plant (GroupShuffleSplit, random_state = 42). See Phase 2.
  • Healthy or sick. Logistic Regression, KNN, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting, all trained on the 26 numbers.
  • Which disease. KNN (k = 7) on the 6 classes.
  • How much damage. Linear Regression predicts the rust level (1 to 4), trained only on rust photos.
  • Where is the damage. K-Means runs on every new photo, on the pixels of the leaf only.
  • What to do. Q-Learning runs in a simulated coffee plot. It learns one table for resistant and one for susceptible varieties.

What the experiments show

  • The three tree models are close to each other (about 82–83.7%) and far above Logistic Regression (up to 78.5%) and KNN (up to 71.2%). The features have non-linear patterns that the simple models miss.
  • Changing the settings of Random Forest moved the score by less than 1.5 points (81.96% to 83.40%). The model is not very sensitive to them.
  • KNN did not improve with more neighbors: the numbers are not good for a distance-based model, even when standardized.

Domain knowledge in the decisions

  • Split by plant. RoCoLe has 4 photos of each plant, and photos of one plant look alike. A plain random split puts a plant in both train and test, and the score looks better than it is. My first comparisons did that, so I replaced them and report only grouped results.
  • Healthy or sick, not the disease. The 6 classes are very unbalanced (791 healthy and only 30 of rust level 4). Grouping them gives two classes of almost the same size (791 and 769).
  • Colors that match the disease. Rust shows as yellow-orange spots that become brown, so the features measure those shares. K-Means works in the Lab color space (channels a and b) because in red-green-blue the result was dominated by brightness and the median damage was 0 in every class. I used 5 groups and a vote of 30%: a group counts as damage if at least 30% of its pixels are yellow, orange or brown.
  • Same cut as the app. The first models used the expert’s outline. The app cuts the leaf by itself, which gave different numbers. All models here use the automatic cut, so the results match what the user gets.
  • Varieties. The Q-Learning rewards follow Cenicafé: resistant varieties do not need chemical control, and susceptible ones should be treated when the rust level grows.
