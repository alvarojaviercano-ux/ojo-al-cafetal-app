Metrics and why

  • Accuracy is the main number. After grouping, the two classes are almost equal (50.7% healthy, 49.3% sick), so accuracy is not fooled by one big class. The goal from Phase 1 was more than 80%.
  • Recall of the sick class. Missing a sick leaf costs more than a false alarm, so I check how many sick leaves are found.
  • Precision, F1 and ROC AUC complete the view: how many alarms are real, the balance of both, and how well the model ranks sick above healthy.
  • Confusion matrix and accuracy per class show where the mistakes are.
  • R² and mean error for the rust level (regression).

Selected model

Random Forest, 300 trees, default settings (the model in the app). 83.0% accuracy on the independent test set, above the 80% goal.

The criterion was the mean accuracy in grouped cross-validation, and the test set was not used to choose. Gradient Boosting had the highest score (83.65% against 83.25%), but the gap is 0.4 points and the difference between folds is about 2 points, so the models are tied. On the test set both got 83.0%. I kept the Random Forest because it was already deployed and checked in the app, and because its score changed very little with its settings (12 settings between 81.96% and 83.40%). This is a decision I made after seeing the table, not a result of the test.

Overfitting, underfitting and bias

  • Overfitting. The Random Forest scores 100% on the photos it trained on, but 83.3% in cross-validation and 83.0% on the test set. The model memorizes its training photos, but cross-validation and test agree, so 83% is an honest estimate for new plants. The learning curve shows the gap between training and validation.
  • Underfitting. Logistic Regression and KNN score only 79–81% even on the photos they trained on, so they are limited by what a simple model can do with these numbers, not by lack of data.
  • Bias. The model catches more sick leaves (85.7%) than it clears healthy ones (80.6%), so it leans toward “sick”. For a farmer this is the safer mistake, but it means false alarms.
  • Data bias. All photos are robusta. The result may be lower for arabica and for other cameras (see the risks below).

K-Means: where is the damage?

K-Means groups the pixels of the leaf by color. A group counts as damage if most of its pixels are yellow, orange or brown. The app paints these pixels in magenta and gives the percent of the leaf.

Rust level in RoCoLeTypical K-Means result

The order is right, but K-Means gives lower numbers than the expert scale. It also misses rust level 1. So the app calls it an estimate of the zone with signs of damage, not a measure of damage.

Q-Learning: what to do?

I made a simulation of one coffee plot. The agent can keep watching, improve crop management, or apply fungicide. The rewards follow Cenicafé’s guidance: resistant varieties (about 80% of the planted area) do not need chemical control, and susceptible ones like Caturra should be treated when the rust level grows.

Honest note: the simulation numbers (costs and rewards) are my assumptions. The advice is a help, not a diagnosis, and a technician should confirm it.

Risk analysis

RISK

Other diseases

The model only knows rust and red spider mite. A leaf with white mold or other spots can still be called “sick”.

RISK

Messy photos

If there are several leaves in the photo, the automatic cut can include more than one. The app asks for one leaf on a plain background and warns when the cut looks wrong.

RISK

Other coffee types

The training photos are robusta. In a few tests with other photos the answers were right, but the confidence was only 60–70%. A real test with arabica is still needed.

RISK

Rust level 1

Very early rust is the hardest case (76.5% correct): the spots are small, and K-Means often finds almost nothing.

RISK

False alarms

32 of 165 healthy test leaves were called sick. The advice asks the user to check more leaves before acting.
